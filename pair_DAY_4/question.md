# Day 4 Question — Evaluation and Statistics

I trained a Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct judge on 69 preference pairs and validated it with Delta B = +0.3204 — the adapter scores held-out outputs significantly higher than the raw backbone. I used that result to conclude the judge learned the Tenacious rubric. But my chosen outputs were Claude Sonnet rewrites of failed Week 10 emails, and my rejected outputs were the original agent outputs that failed the rubric. Claude rewrites are structurally more elaborate than the original outputs. I cannot explain whether my judge learned to identify rubric compliance — or whether it learned that longer, more elaborate outputs score higher, because that pattern was present in every preference pair it trained on. Delta B = +0.32 looks identical under both explanations, and I do not know what I would need to observe in the judge's scoring behavior to tell them apart.

**The question:** When an LLM judge is trained on preference pairs where chosen outputs are systematically more elaborate than rejected ones, what specific pattern in the judge's scores on held-out outputs would reveal that it learned verbosity bias rather than rubric compliance — and what would that pattern look like in my existing `ablations/ablation_results.json`?

**Grounded in:** `ablations/ablation_results.json` (Delta B: mean_lift=0.3204, wins=37/40, n=40 tasks) — the result I reported as evidence the judge learned the rubric — and `methodology_rationale.md`, which describes chosen outputs as Claude Sonnet rewrites and rejected outputs as original agent failures, but contains no analysis of whether the two groups differ in length or structural elaborateness. A satisfying answer names a specific diagnostic signal I could compute from the 40 scored outputs I already have, without re-running training.

**Why it generalizes:** Every FDE who trains a small judge on preference pairs and validates it with an aggregate score lift faces this exact ambiguity. A judge that rewards elaborateness rather than correctness will quietly inflate scores on verbose production outputs while missing genuine rubric failures — and the standard ablation result cannot distinguish the two. Knowing what signal breaks the tie is the judgment call that separates a trustworthy evaluation component from one that just looks good on paper.
